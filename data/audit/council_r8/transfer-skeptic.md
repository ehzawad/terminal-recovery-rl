## Skeptic: validity of the transfer claim and the evaluation (transfer-skeptic)

**I would not launch the 45-GPU-hour four-arm study now.** There are material problems in this lens: the proposed external evaluation is not yet valid, the convenient offline subset has a pronounced selection effect, development use could consume the final evaluation, and the promotion rule has weak power for gains near its threshold. A bounded, model-free admission and harness audit is worth doing if the owner wants to preserve this option. The present evidence supports “transfer is possible,” but does not make “r16 LoRA beats each of P, S and D by 12 points within 45 GPU-hours” a well-supported expectation.

I inspected the pinned task repositories, the registration and current verifier, and the published sources. I ran static inventories, synthetic power calculations, and one isolated grader reproduction. I ran no model, training job, Docker build, or full benchmark reference solution. Thus, the runtime validity and cost of the proposed external evaluation remain unverified. The [inventory and calculations](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/terminal-recovery-rl/data/audit/council_r8/external-evaluation/council-r8-external-validity.json) and [reproduction record](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/terminal-recovery-rl/data/audit/council_r8/external-evaluation/council-r8-grader-repro.json) are saved for reconciliation.

The strongest concrete finding is a **score interpretation mismatch**. Forty TBLite tasks use pytest wrappers that write the actual grader score separately and then pass their pytest assertion regardless of that score. For example, [grpc-plant-position-server/tests/test_outputs.py:26](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/.pools/tblite/grpc-plant-position-server/tests/test_outputs.py:26) writes `result.score`, while line 45 executes `assert True`. The existing ET verifier defines success through passing its pytest inventory, as documented in [verify.py:9](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/terminal-recovery-rl/termrl/verify.py:9). Adapting filenames while retaining that interpretation would award success to unsuccessful tasks. Some TBLite graders also award fractional scores: [tsl-test-case-generation/tests/grader.py:59](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/.pools/tblite/tsl-test-case-generation/tests/grader.py:59) combines weighted subscores. The study must distinguish native benchmark reward, complete task success, and pytest execution status. Binarizing native fractional rewards creates a different metric and must be declared.

There is also a verified weak-grader example inside the proposed offline pool. In [publisher-market-analysis-v2/tests/grader.py:45](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/.pools/tblite/publisher-market-analysis-v2/tests/grader.py:45), a missing required database increments a failure counter, but any CSV parsed as zero rows subsequently returns score 1 without checking that counter or the required columns. I imported the original grader, redirected only its `/workdir` paths into a temporary directory, and supplied `wrong_header\n` with no database. It returned **1.0**, while its feedback acknowledged the missing database. This was an isolated grader reproduction, not an end-to-end container test. It establishes that reference-pass/no-op-fail checks alone are insufficient: an empty untouched environment could fail while a trivial, substantively incomplete submission passes.

The current filesystem-snapshot grading approach also cannot preserve every task’s required state. The gRPC task explicitly requires a background server, and its grader checks a live listening port and makes RPC calls: [instruction.md:10](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/.pools/tblite/grpc-plant-position-server/instruction.md:10), [grader.py:145](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/.pools/tblite/grpc-plant-position-server/tests/grader.py:145). A filesystem snapshot does not preserve running processes. Automatically starting a server during grading could instead rescue an agent that failed to start it. Other tasks deliberately restart services in their verifier. Those semantics need task-specific preservation. The existing sandbox additionally overrides the image entrypoint and applies its own user, workdir and ownership behavior in [sandbox.py:95](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/terminal-recovery-rl/termrl/sandbox.py:95). A longer context window does not resolve these compatibility problems.

The network scouting is directionally correct but needs narrower wording. All 89 pinned TB2.1 tasks enable internet, and all 89 verifier entrypoints contain dependency-installation commands. They do not all use `uvx`: [hf-model-inference/tests/test.sh:4](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/.pools/tb21/tasks/hf-model-inference/tests/test.sh:4) uses pip. These are static dependencies, not proof that every grader must contact the internet after proper provisioning. Conversely, a grader lacking installation commands is not proof that the entire task works offline. Agent downloads, helper programs, image construction, local services, and transitive dependencies must be inspected separately. For example, [hf-model-inference/instruction.md:3](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/.pools/tb21/tasks/hf-model-inference/instruction.md:3) explicitly requires downloading model weights.

**The 44-task subset is particularly vulnerable to selection effects.** My static inventory reproduces 44 tasks when broadly filtering network-looking commands. Forty of those 44 are precisely the tasks with the grader-wrapper pattern above; those same forty use an Ubuntu 22.04 base and one-hour agent timeouts. The selected set contains five tasks labeled easy, versus 24 in the full 100-task repository. These are descriptive metadata differences, not calibrated Qwen difficulty estimates, but they show that the filter selects a packaging and source-style stratum. It is not approximately random removal of internet dependencies. There is even a filtering false positive: `auth_token_race_condition` uses curl for a localhost health check, with dependencies installed in the image, so distinguishing external downloads from loopback communication produces 45 initial candidates. Neither count is an admitted-task count.

This selection could bias the transfer result in either direction. Keeping local data-processing tasks might favor ET-trained behavior; retaining long-running, unusual grader tasks might depress it. Bootstrap intervals cannot correct that selection. Freeze the eligibility rules before model outcomes, publish the entire inclusion/exclusion ledger, and describe the estimand as performance on the audited offline subset under the specified harness. Do not call its score the canonical TBLite or TB2.1 score, and do not infer full-benchmark performance from it. Grader-only dependencies should first be packaged offline where feasible, so an arbitrary installation style does not determine membership. Preinstalling a dependency that the agent is explicitly tasked with installing, however, can remove part of the problem.

Task count also overstates independence unless related tasks are grouped. The two publisher-market-analysis instructions have 98.2% character similarity in my check and differ principally in a filtering threshold: [original instruction](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/.pools/tblite/publisher-market-analysis/instruction.md:27), [v2 instruction](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/.pools/tblite/publisher-market-analysis-v2/instruction.md:25). They require joint lineage adjudication and must not straddle development and final evaluation. Shared Dockerfiles alone do not make tasks one statistical cluster; shared underlying problems can. The absence of identical task-directory names between TBLite and TB2.1 is insufficient evidence of independence, as is lexical decontamination alone between ET training and external evaluation.

The published benchmark’s history reinforces the need for admission checks. The official TB2.1 release reports fixes to 28 of 89 tasks, including external dependency drift, resource budgets and instruction/test disagreement. Its reported score changes reach 12.1 points for one unchanged agent-model combination—approximately the entire proposed promotion margin. This does not establish that the pinned 2.1 tasks remain broken; it establishes that environment and specification defects can move this metric materially. [Official TB2.1 release](https://www.tbench.ai/news/terminal-bench-2-1)

Before **any candidate-model run, including P**, every admitted external task needs an end-to-end positive control and negative control under the exact intended evaluation setup. The reference must achieve the defined complete-success outcome on repeated fresh resets; the no-op must fail it, with any partial reward explained. Reference execution must use the intended agent privileges and resources, and must not succeed through reference-only answer access or network privileges unavailable to the model. Check actual test collection and execution, native reward parsing, missing or stale reward files, service startup, timeouts, and repeatability. Run these checks with representative shared-host concurrency, not only in an otherwise idle single container.

Admission also needs instruction/test review and targeted negative submissions. Remove or corrupt required outputs, omit required database contents, stop a required service, and try a trivial placeholder where applicable. The publisher-v2 reproduction demonstrates why this matters. Audit references and images for exposed solutions, hidden requirements, time-dependent fixtures and tests that merely check filenames or strings. A passing reference proves one route through the implementation; it does not prove that the grader measures the stated task or accepts other valid solutions. These checks should be registered and applied without consulting candidate-model success rates.

Dependency freezing must cover more than the top-level pytest version. Record task commits, image digests, interpreter versions, direct and transitive packages, downloaded fixtures and model revisions, plus the runner and score-aggregation implementation. A dated image tag or `ubuntu:22.04` is not an immutable identity. Provision images and dependency caches before timed evaluation; retain content-addressed images and the artifacts needed for offline replay. Verify fresh-container execution with external networking disabled and without relying on an undocumented warm user cache. Grader-only packages should remain unavailable to the agent when exposing them would change the task. Reference runs must never populate the agent’s starting image with solutions or successful output artifacts.

The admission record must explicitly identify every departure from the upstream environment: internet policy, user and capabilities, startup commands, workdir, filesystem mounts, CPU/RAM, interpreter, output truncation, tool interface, command timeout, context handling and total episode timeout. Preserve loopback services where the task requires them. Validate that hidden tests and trusted reward reporting cannot be replaced by agent-written artifacts. The old `/home/user` collateral oracle cannot certify tasks modifying `/app`, `/workdir`, services or system configuration. Either develop appropriate contracts and guardrails or limit the claim to task performance; benchmark success alone does not establish safe terminal execution or a recovery mechanism.

A modified harness can support a valid **internal** comparison when all four arms receive the same frozen treatment. It does not support direct comparison with published numbers. Training at the existing short horizon and evaluating at a longer horizon additionally tests a particular form of transfer, which should be explicit. Do not shorten budgets or disable useful capabilities merely to obtain P failures. Genuine headroom means valid, solvable tasks that a competitive baseline cannot reliably complete, not failures created by infrastructure restrictions. Unexpected infrastructure failures need a registered, outcome-blind adjudication and symmetric rerun policy; they must not become whichever arm’s “capability failures” happen to encounter them.

On contamination, TBLite explicitly presents itself as a development benchmark for debugging, iteration and ablations, recommending TB2 for final validation. [TBLite README](https://github.com/open-thoughts/OpenThoughts-TBLite) Any task used to select prompts, checkpoints, context limits, stopping steps, training mixtures or other choices is consumed for development. A headroom gate is also development feedback. It cannot subsequently be counted as untouched final evidence. More rollout seeds do not restore an exhausted task to holdout status.

There are two defensible designs. Freeze the entire recipe using ET development data and take a single final reading on a previously unused external set; or use external development tasks and reserve a separate, lineage-disjoint final set before any model run. The second design makes the already small final denominator smaller. A prepartitioned untouched TBLite portion can support a local held-out pilot, but it is not the full published TBLite evaluation. Treating all TBLite as development and TB2.1 as final is cleaner conceptually, but the offline admission and compute requirements for that final gate are currently unproven. “Possibly TB2.1 as a secondary” needs resolution before results are observed.

The published TMax result supports the direction of transfer, with important limits. Its ET-trained Qwen3.5-9B improves TBLite from 41.9 to 52.6 and TB2.1 from 16.1 to 25.5. The recipe uses 500 steps, eight prompts with 32 rollouts each, up to 65,536 tokens and 64 tool calls, and H100-cluster training. It selects checkpoints using TBLite every 100 steps. Evaluation uses Harbor, default task timeouts and three runs; the authors explicitly discuss backend and inference-speed effects. Its reported uncertainties are standard errors across those runs. These are not results for this LoRA pilot or comparisons against matched P, S and D. [TMax, experimental setup and Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.23321v1#S4)

My inference is that **some positive transfer is plausible; the required +12 over all three controls is a poor planning assumption**. The cited +10.7 is already below the target even before accounting for an improved baseline. If the strongest control gained five points over raw weights, a comparable R would need at least seventeen points over raw weights. That is an illustration, not a forecast. Checkpoint selection can add optimism to TBLite performance, although it does not establish that the gain is spurious. Nor is full training an upper bound on LoRA. The correct conclusion is that the paper provides no calibration for this much smaller-budget, narrower-data, different-objective comparison. In particular, it does not show that the gain survives the D control.

The statistical limitation is substantial but should not be exaggerated into “40–90 tasks can never detect anything.” For paired binary outcomes, let \(q\) be the probability that R and a control disagree and \(\delta\) their true success-rate difference. With one attempt per independent task, the contrast’s standard error is approximately \(\sqrt{(q-\delta^2)/n}\). At \(\delta=0.12\), \(q=0.40\), that is 9.8 points for 40 tasks and 6.5 points for 90. An observed twelve-point difference therefore has wide uncertainty.

I calculated exact rejection probabilities for the point-estimate gate and the ideal, infinite-resample percentile-bootstrap gate together. Under that illustrative \(q=0.40\), independent-task, one-attempt model, a true **+12** clears both gates against one control with probabilities **34%, 46% and 52%** for 40, 60 and 90 tasks. A true **+20** gives approximately **67%, 83% and 91%**. These are sensitivity calculations, not forecasts of the proposed study. They exclude mechanism and guardrail requirements and training-seed variation. The [calculation script](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/terminal-recovery-rl/data/audit/council_r8/external-evaluation/council-r8-external-validity.py) makes the assumptions explicit.

Repeats help, but do not create new independent tasks. With four attempts and an assumed within-task correlation of paired contrasts of 0.5, a normal approximation gives only about 45%–50% probability of clearing both numerical gates at a true +12 across 40–90 tasks. The reason is structural: even with improving precision, an estimator centered on twelve clears a twelve-point point-estimate threshold only about half the time. Detecting a positive effect, clearing a lower bound above three, and reliably exceeding twelve are different objectives. Requiring success against all controls can only reduce the marginal promotion probability; its exact cost depends on their joint correlation through R.

The current implementation’s “80% interval” uses the 10th and 90th percentiles, so its lower endpoint is a one-sided 90% bound: [analyze.py:54](/mnt/sdb/arafat/ehz/llm/terminal-recovery-rl/scripts/analyze.py:54). Do not silently switch conventions. Requiring every comparison to pass is an intersection-union claim, so three controls do not automatically require a Bonferroni correction for that single claim. That does not protect against selecting subsets, prompts or checkpoints on final outcomes. Also, a point estimate above twelve with a lower bound above three does **not** demonstrate that the true improvement exceeds twelve. Rare collateral events create a further limitation: an all-zero sample can yield a degenerate bootstrap interval without establishing a one-point safety margin.

Compute feasibility is likewise unestablished. The 44 preliminary TBLite candidates have agent timeouts totaling 41 serial hours per pass. If attempts reach those limits, four arms with four attempts require **at least 82 wall-hours at eight perfectly utilized sandbox slots**, before grading and other overhead. That is a stress scenario, not an expected runtime. But retaining the previous study’s 6.5-hour final-evaluation allocation would require an average below 266 seconds per trial for 44 tasks, or 130 seconds for 90 tasks, even with ideal eight-way utilization. The roughly 49-second ET measurement cannot justify those external-task assumptions. Prompt search, checkpoint evaluation, S and R collection and fitting, D, retention and contingency all share the 45-hour ceiling.

The dependencies on the other council work are therefore concrete: a validated state-preserving external evaluator; a model-independent admission ledger and lineage split; a prospective mechanism and safety definition; and measured throughput sufficient to finish every arm and the frozen final evaluation. Until those exist, no defensible number of independent final tasks or complete-study cost is available. If they do exist, the likely claim remains a single-training-seed pilot on an audited offline subset. An unsuccessful promotion decision would often be inconclusive rather than evidence of no useful gain.

Stopping here would preserve the valid public contribution. The current [public dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ehzawad/terminal-recovery-bench) already says the planned RL comparison did not proceed and limits the results to the evaluated ET slice. A stop supplies no evidence that RL fixed terminal recovery, beats SFT, transfers externally or cannot help. The public outcome should distinguish the earlier prompting results, the hard-task admission failure, and the decision not to launch an external comparison without adequate validity and feasibility. Unless a new registered gate is actually executed, this is a decision not to launch Study 4—not a fourth completed negative experiment.

The remaining owner decisions are whether a carefully scoped exploratory transfer pilot is valuable despite low promotion power; whether TBLite will be development or final material; and whether to fund the admission and evaluator work before committing GPU time. My recommendation is to stop before model runs, permit only a capped model-free readiness audit if desired, and keep the public “RL demonstrably fixed it” claim unmade.
